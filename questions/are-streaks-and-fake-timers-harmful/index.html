Are streaks and fake timers in kids' apps harmful?

Ages 3–12 · time design

There is a large trial showing streaks will keep a child using an app for longer. Countdown timers, while common, aren’t tested yet with children.

1What the research says

The claim tested

“Streaks and countdown timers that push play or purchases make children use apps longer than they otherwise would”

Mixed evidenceMixed evidence: good studies disagree, or the claim is true only for some children.

The answer could easily change.LikelyLikely: several studies point the same way, and at least one has a strong design.EstablishedEstablished: many studies with strong designs agree, and reviews confirm it.

Why it’s mixed

The evidence is mixed. Streaks were tested with children and increased the time they used the apps. Countdown timers weren’t tested yet with children, only with adults, with mixed results.

What the studies found

Countdown timers and streaks are tools in what’s called persuasive design, a way to make users follow the app’s goals more closely. Timers haven’t been studied yet with children, even though they’re common in children’s apps. Streaks will make children use apps longer, as one trial focused on a maths app showed.

Also found

  • Problem phone use went up. Teens with more and longer Snapchat streaks reported slightly more fear of missing out and problems with phone use. The links were weak, from one survey. Snapchat streaks

2In practice

  • TestedStudies tested this directly.

    Persuasive design particularly affects children who find it harder to stop, keeping them playing longer. Notice how easily your child stops when using an app that has streaks or countdown timers.

  • Nearby evidenceNo study tested this, but studies of adults, other designs or the same mechanism point this way.

    Talk to them about what a broken streak or lapse means, so keeping the streak alive doesn’t become the goal instead of playing or learning.

  • PrecautionNot measured yet. Worth doing anyway: it’s common, there’s a known way it could do harm, and it’s easy to avoid.

    The effects of countdown timers aren’t yet measured, but keep in mind they’re another tool an app can use to keep children playing longer.

3The evidence7 findings

What’s behind the claim

Streaks make children use a learning app more

Early signs it holdsHolds: the research mostly supports the claim. Early signs: one or two studies, or studies that show a link but maybe not a cause. The answer could easily change.

Holds: the research mostly supports the claim.

Pupils in grades 4–6 who were shown their streaks used a maths app more than pupils who got no messages, and about as much as with personal reminders. One trial, in Peru.

Countdown timers make children play longer

Not studied yetNot studied yet: no study has tested this, so there’s no evidence either way.

Not studied yet: no study has tested this, so there’s no evidence either way.

No study has measured what countdown timers do to children. One small trial put them in its most persuasive app, among many other features, so their own effect can't be told apart.

What changes the answer

Things like a child’s age, or whether a parent plays along.

Children who find it hard to stop are affected more

Early signs it holdsHolds: the research mostly supports the claim. Early signs: one or two studies, or studies that show a link but maybe not a cause. The answer could easily change.

Holds: the research mostly supports the claim.

With more persuasive apps, 3–5 year olds with low self-control took longer to stop and needed help. The others stopped easily either way. The most persuasive app had countdown timers among many other features. The difference showed on one of two self-control measures.

In learning apps, streaks keep children learning

Mixed evidenceMixed evidence: good studies disagree, or the claim is true only for some children.

Mixed evidence: good studies disagree, or the claim is true only for some children.

The Peru trial found better maths scores. In interviews, 50 Duolingo families described children keeping the streak alive after the learning had stopped.

Countdown timers push adults to buy

Mixed evidenceMixed evidence: good studies disagree, or the claim is true only for some children.

Mixed evidence: good studies disagree, or the claim is true only for some children.

In a large US experiment, a one-minute countdown on an offer didn’t make people sign up.

Why it happens

Possible reasons, each with its own evidence.

People keep going so they don’t break a streak

Early signs it holdsHolds: the research mostly supports the claim. Early signs: one or two studies, or studies that show a link but maybe not a cause. The answer could easily change.

Holds: the research mostly supports the claim.

In seven experiments with adults, an unbroken streak made people more likely to continue than a broken one. Not tested with children.

Children feel they must keep a streak alive

Early signs it holdsHolds: the research mostly supports the claim. Early signs: one or two studies, or studies that show a link but maybe not a cause. The answer could easily change.

Holds: the research mostly supports the claim.

In interviews, children aged 11–13 described getting up early, eating while playing or asking a sibling to keep a streak going, and blamed themselves when it broke. Nobody counted how common this is.

4Age and details

On age

Research was done with 3–5, 9–12 and 11–13 year olds. A further experiment was with adults. Streaks under 9 years weren't tested, and there's only a weak link in a study with teens. There's no research comparing across age ranges, so there's no evidence the answer differs by age.
