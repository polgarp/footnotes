How I judge the research

I strive to follow a single and consistent approach when answering questions on this site. This page describes how.

Who writes this

I’m a parent and a designer who spends time reading scientific studies to better understand the real evidence, and to make better choices based on data, beyond anecdotes or a vague intuition. I’m not a scientist, so while I strive to be rigorous, my approach might be more pragmatic on occasion.

How questions are chosen

The list of questions is short and carefully selected for a reason: I want to make sure I spend enough time on each. That said, I welcome questions beyond our immediate family, from friends and from you, dear reader.

From a question to a claim

A question like “Are streaks harmful?” can’t be tested as it stands: it’s too vague to compare with the studies done. So each page translates the question into a more precise claim, worded so the corresponding studies can prove it right or wrong.

Words like “harmful”, “safe” or “better” can mean several things. Before I search, I write down what the word could mean for this question. The claim reformulates the question into a more concrete statement based on these. I also note smaller claims, to make it clear what has evidence and what’s missing it. Sometimes the search turns up a new direction I haven’t considered, and I take note of that too in the evidence section.

How I find the research

I check the likely sources: reviews that already exist, research databases, working papers, and reports from governments and organisations. I follow the references in each good study, look up the newer studies that cite it, and search on purpose for studies that disagree.

I only use established sources: journals listed in the main research databases, known working-paper series, and official bodies. I don’t use statistics websites or summaries written by AI tools.

How I check what I quote

I copy the quotes on the site from the source papers, and note where in the paper they come from. This way I can go beyond AI summaries, back to the original starting point.

What the verdicts mean

The verdict says whether the research supports the claim behind the question.

  • Holds. The research mostly supports the claim.
  • Doesn’t hold. The research mostly shows little or no effect, or the opposite effect.
  • Mixed evidence. Good studies disagree, or the claim is true only for some children.
  • Not studied yet. I found no study connected to the claim.

Sometimes I find research that isn’t about the claim, but is connected to it. These results show next to the answer as “Also found”, with an arrow for the direction of what the study found.

How settled the evidence is

The verdict’s position on the line shows how settled the evidence is.

  • Early signs. One or two studies, or studies that show a link but maybe not a cause. The answer could easily change.
  • Likely. Several studies point the same way, and at least one has a strong design. Some questions are still open.
  • Established. Many studies with strong designs agree, and reviews of the research confirm it. New studies are unlikely to change the answer.

In both verdicts and evidence I use words instead of exact scores, since scores might suggest stronger evidence than we actually have.

What “In practice” means

“In practice” lists the choices the evidence leaves you with. This isn’t my advice, but a summary of what to know, based on the evidence for the question.

The choices depend on the evidence I found:

  • Tested. A study tested this directly.
  • Nearby evidence. No study tested this, but studies of adults, other designs or the same mechanism point this way.
  • Precaution. Not measured yet. I only suggest it when the thing is common, there’s a known way it could do harm, and it’s easy to avoid.
  • Up to you. The research doesn’t support a claim either way, so it’s up to your family’s values and situation.

Nearby evidence never changes a verdict. A verdict rests only on studies that tested the claim itself.

The evidence for each question

Each question page splits the claim into smaller claims, each with its own verdict and the study it rests on. They come in up to three groups:

  • What’s behind the claim. The parts of the claim I set before searching.
  • What changes the answer. Things like a child’s age, or whether a parent plays along.
  • Why it happens. Possible reasons, each with its own evidence.

Each smaller claim also shows the quote from the paper it rests on.

At the end of each question page, the Sources section lists every study for the question.

Types of study

Some kinds of study can show more than others. These are the standard names researchers use, from strongest to weakest for questions like these:

  1. Reviews. Find every study on a question with a systematic search. Meta-analyses also pool the results into one estimate.
  2. Randomised controlled trials. Participants are put into groups at random, so the groups differ only in what’s being tested.
  3. Quasi-experimental studies. Compare groups without random assignment. For example, schools before and after a new rule, or families using different versions of an app.
  4. Cohort studies. Follow the same people over time. Can show what comes first, but not always what causes what.
  5. Cross-sectional studies. Measure a group at one point in time. Can show a link, but not a cause.
  6. Technical audits. Researchers test devices or apps directly for security and privacy flaws. Show what can go wrong, not how often it does.
  7. Qualitative studies. Interviews and open-ended surveys. Show what people think and why. Weak for “what works”.
  8. Narrative reviews and reports. Overviews and arguments without a systematic search, including reports from governments and organisations. I check the studies they rely on.

How I read a study

I try to be efficient when reading papers: I focus on the abstract, methodology and discussion, and occasionally dive deeper into the details to understand specific points. This allows me to focus on the findings and find the right quotes.

What this site is not

This site isn’t medical advice. I mostly do it to understand things better myself, so there are no ads and I’m not seeking sponsors. If you’re worried about how your child is doing, I suggest always talking to them first and foremost, and seeking advice from their kindergarten, school or other experts.

Corrections and updates

Each question page shows the date it was last published, and I update pages when I find new information. For corrections and mistakes, please email me at hello@polgarp.com.
